---
title: "The Crate Manifesto"
description: "Why Crate exists: streaming platforms extract value from artists and listeners, and small communities can run their own music infrastructure instead."
---

A while ago, I stopped feeling comfortable using the major streaming platforms. At first it was a vague discomfort, a sense that something no longer fit. Over time the reasons became painfully clear: how they treat artists, the ruthless extraction, and business decisions that couldn't be separated from their political and cultural consequences. Eventually the question stopped being "which service do I use?" and became "what kind of system am I feeding?"

<p class="em">Music has always meant everything to me: discovery, obsession, context, memory, identity, community.</p>

It got harder to reconcile that with platforms that treat music as background noise, push algorithm-driven playlists built to keep me hooked for five more minutes, and run the whole thing as a supply chain optimized for attention and corporate profit.

People say the system is broken. It's working exactly as designed: it takes value from artists and moves it upward. An independent musician needs nearly four hundred thousand streams to earn one month of US minimum wage, at fractions of a cent per play, while the platforms hand billions to shareholders. The pro-rata model is a legal scam: your listens subsidize major-label hits and executive bonuses.

Spotify is the largest extractor. It pays roughly half of what Apple Music pays per stream, and since 2024 it pays no recording royalties at all on any track with fewer than a thousand streams a year. Meanwhile it spent over a billion dollars chasing podcast exclusives, and its founder's investment fund led a €600 million round into Helsing, a military AI and strike-drone company he chairs. Apple Music and Amazon Music are loss leaders that exist to sell hardware and Prime subscriptions; the music is bait. YouTube Music monetizes your attention and serves ads on independent artists' work without asking them. These are extraction companies that happen to sell access to music.

<p class="em">In a system like this, piracy is not theft. It's self-defense.</p>

That doesn't mean you stop paying artists. It means you stop paying middlemen. Pirate the catalog the labels hold hostage, the records that already made their money ten times over, and spend every cent you save on concert tickets, Bandcamp purchases, merch tables and direct support. Pirate from the corporation and pay the artist, always both.

The real theft happens every day, in plain sight, through terms of service and quarterly earnings reports. A platform pays an artist $0.003 per stream and calls it "democratizing music." Its algorithms bury independent musicians under an endless sea of AI-generated filler and major-label playlist placements. And the whole relationship between a listener and a musician runs through a corporation whose only incentive is to keep you scrolling.

That's why more and more artists are saying enough. Deerhoof, Massive Attack, King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard, Godspeed You! Black Emperor, Xiu Xiu and others have pulled their music from Spotify. The payouts were part of it, but they also refused to let their listeners' money enrich a platform whose founder bankrolls military AI.

The math is public. On a Bandcamp Friday, thirty fans buying a ten-dollar album put close to two hundred and eighty dollars directly in the artist's hands. To earn the same from Spotify, that artist needs around ninety thousand streams, and that's before the label and the distributor take their cut. The platforms are counting on you never doing this math.

This is bigger than music. The same logic turns housing into investment vehicles, food delivery into gig exploitation and personal data into surveillance products. Platforms own the pipes, extract the value and leave creators with scraps while calling it opportunity.

Your listening history is part of that trade. Every skip, every repeat, every 3 AM playlist feeds a profile that gets bought and sold. With Crate, that data stays on your server. Nobody builds a profile of your taste, and no algorithm nudges you toward whatever pays the highest royalty.

So yes, download the music and build a library of files you own. Then take the money you would have given a streaming platform and spend it where it matters.

Buy the concert ticket, the record at the merch table, the t-shirt. Send the band a message telling them their album changed your week. Show up. That does more to keep music alive than another passive stream into a pool that pays artists less than minimum wage.

<p class="em">Platforms call music content. It's culture, and culture shouldn't be rented. We're taking it back.</p>

This isn't a solo project either. It's a bet that small groups of people (friends, collectives, local scenes, the people who will still be self-hosting things in ten years) can build their own infrastructure. Run a Crate instance for your community. Host a listening party. Share your library with your housemates. Nobody needs another monolith to replace Spotify; the point is to make it irrelevant, one community server at a time.

<p class="em">Own your music. Support your artists. Refuse the middleman.</p>
